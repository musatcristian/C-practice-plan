Bare-metal and RTOS firmware: a 34-week learning path

Cristian Musat · weeks 4 to 34 · 6 to 8 hours per week · prepared 4 November 2026

How to read this plan

  • Weeks 1 to 3 are yours and are unchanged. This plan starts at week 4 and assumes those three weeks are done as written. Nothing here overwrites them.
  • Every phase ends in an artefact. Not a certificate, not a completed course. A repository, a measurement, or a written analysis that another engineer could inspect without you in the room.
  • Time budget is per week, not per phase. If a phase overruns by two weeks, cut scope inside it rather than dropping the artefact. The artefact is the point.
  • Two gates. Each is a genuine stop point with a defined test. Stopping is a legitimate result, and cheaper in week 14 than in week 30.
  • Sequencing is fixed. Phase 4 depends on Phase 3; Phase 5 depends on a board that boots an RTOS you configured. Do not run phases in parallel except where marked optional.

Objective

By week 34 you can show, from artefacts: production-standard C that has been reviewed by people who do not know you; a board brought up from a linker script you wrote; a real-time pipeline with a measured worst-case latency and a defensible methodology; a firmware-to-Linux boundary you have crossed in both directions; and one repository a firmware hiring manager would read as competent rather than enthusiastic. The target position is a lateral move at current grade into a firmware or embedded Linux team, presented as a systems engineer transferring depth.

Phase 4 · Weeks 4 to 7 · Production C · you can compile; you have never had your C survive review. This phase fixes that, and it is the most common reason people with your profile get filtered at interview.

WeekWorkAcceptance criterion

Fix every warning rather than disabling it.Clean build from a fresh clone on a machine that has never seen the repo. Zero warnings, and a one-line justification committed for any suppressed check.

Phase 5 · Weeks 8 to 11 · Bare-metal bring-up · you already write linker scripts. This phase moves that from a claim into a repository with a commit history, on real silicon.

WeekWorkAcceptance criterion
8Get a toolchain you chose (arm-none-eabi-gcc or clang + newlib/picolibc) building for the target. Read the part's memory map from the datasheet and the reference manual, and write the linker script by hand: flash and RAM regions, section placement, the vector table, stack reservation, and the .data load address versus run address.arm-none-eabi-readelf -S on the ELF shows sections at the addresses the datasheet says, and a committed note explaining each MEMORY region and why the stack sits where it does.
9Startup code end to end: Reset_Handler, copy .data from flash to RAM, zero .bss, set the vector table, jump to main. Optionally in assembly, which is worth it once.A board that reaches main with a verifiable signal (a GPIO toggle or a UART character) with no vendor HAL and no IDE project involved.
10Register-level peripheral work, no HAL: clock enable, GPIO configuration, and one interrupt-driven peripheral of your choice. Write the IRQ handler and the vector entry yourself.An interrupt fires and is serviced, with a GPIO toggle or captured analyser edge as proof, and a short written explanation of the NVIC vector lookup and the ISR contract.
11Deliberately break it and document it. Introduce a fault (unaligned access, invalid pointer, divide by zero), capture the fault status registers, and write a minimal hard-fault handler that prints the stacked PC and LR.A fault handler that identifies the offending instruction address on a real fault, with the captured registers committed and explained.

Phase 6 · Weeks 12 to 17 · RTOS and timing · the heart of the target role, and the phase almost everyone skips the measurement half of.

WeekWorkAcceptance criterion
12Bring up Zephyr (or FreeRTOS) on your board using its own board support, then write a second, minimal port-aware note: what devicetree contributes, what Kconfig contributes, and where your hand-written startup code and the RTOS's port layer meet.A Zephyr or FreeRTOS image running on the board, plus a diagram you drew of how your Phase 5 code relates to the RTOS port.
13Concurrency primitives: tasks at different priorities, a message queue for ISR-to-task handoff, a mutex, and a semaphore. Then break it: create a priority inversion and observe it.A demonstrated priority inversion, the fix (priority inheritance or better design), and a measurement showing the fix works.
14Gate 1. Build the measurement harness: a GPIO toggle at task entry and exit, captured on the logic analyser, to get ISR-to-task latency and jitter. Run it under load.A committed measurement report stating the deadline, the observed worst case, the number of runs, and the conditions. If you cannot produce this, stop the plan here.
15Scheduling theory applied to your own numbers: rate monotonic and fixed-priority analysis on your task set, CPU utilisation, and whether your deadlines are provably met rather than empirically observed.A written schedulability analysis for your task set, with the assumptions stated and the conclusion honest (including "not provable, here is why").
16Memory discipline without a heap: static allocation, fixed-size pools, stack sizing from a high-water mark, and why malloc is often banned. Measure your real stack usage.Removal of all dynamic allocation from the firmware with task stack high-water marks reported and sized with margin.
17Safety and robustness habits: MISRA-C awareness on your own code, a watchdog with a documented recovery policy, and a defensive check on every external input.A clang-tidy MISRA-adjacent run with zero unjustified deviations, and a watchdog that demonstrably recovers from a deliberate hang.

Gate 1 · end of week 14 (run it, then continue).

Pass if: you have a measured worst-case ISR-to-task latency with a stated method, and you found the work absorbing rather than merely correct.

Stop if: no measurement exists, or you did not enjoy it. Keep the full stack base, which is now stronger rather than eroded, and do not buy the Linux board.

Phase 7 · Weeks 18 to 20 · Protocols at the wire · the skill that makes you useful on a real product team, and cheaper than any certification at teaching you what firmware teams actually test.

WeekWorkAcceptance criterion
18SPI and I2C properly: read a real device's datasheet, drive it from scratch, decode both on the logic analyser, and produce a timing diagram from your capture next to the one in the datasheet.A decoded capture matched against the datasheet timing, with your own byte-level commentary. This is the artefact that proves bench competence.
19CAN at the wire level: framing, arbitration, IDs, error handling. Two nodes if you have a second transceiver, or a loopback with a simulated peer. Discussion of CAN FD.Frames captured and decoded, an arbitration experiment, and an explanation of how a real system would handle error states.
20Cross-compile a Linux image for the second board with Yocto or Buildroot. Add a layer or a recipe. Write a devicetree overlay from scratch.An image you built reproducibly, booting, with your overlay applied and a node visible in /proc/device-tree.

Phase 8 · Weeks 21 to 26 · Firmware to Linux boundary · the bridge profile: you can go downward into the kernel and upward into the application, and you know where the seam belongs.

WeekWorkAcceptance criterion
21Kernel module basics on your board: a loadable module, a character device, read/write or ioctl on it, correct error paths and cleanup.A driver loaded on the target with a userspace program talking to it, and a clean unload with no leaked resources.
22Concurrency in the kernel: spinlocks, mutexes, sleeping contexts, deferred work, and why a bug here is nondeterministic. Trigger a race and find it.A race deliberately introduced and then diagnosed, with the reasoning written down rather than the patch alone.
23A small, honest kernel patch: fix something in a subsystem you actually touched, ideally something reported and small. Follow the subsystem's submission process.One patch sent upstream with reviewer feedback, or a well-argued explanation of why it was rejected. Either is evidence of real work.
24Real-time on Linux: PREEMPT_RT configuration, interrupt threading, and a cyclictest run with interpretation of the latency distribution.Latency numbers with an explanation of what dominates them and what a system integrator would tune.
25Build the seam: a small end-to-end system where your firmware talks to your Linux application over SPI or UART, with framed messages and a defined protocol.Two devices, one protocol, capture evidence, and a written interface specification. This repo is the single best conversation starter you will have.
26Test it like a product team would: host-side unit tests for the protocol logic, a hardware-in-the-loop style test, and a CI job that builds both halves on a clean runner.make verify from clean passes for both halves with no manual steps, and a README a stranger can follow.

Gate 2 · end of week 26.

Pass if: you can explain a bug you fixed at register or kernel level, and the seam system builds and tests from clean.

Not yet: repeat weeks 21 to 23 on a harder subsystem before starting Phase 9. Do not start positioning on a thin artefact.

Phase 9 · Weeks 27 to 30 · The reviewable artefact · one repository that reads like a working firmware engineer's best month, not like a learner's portfolio.

WeekWorkAcceptance criterion
27Consolidate. Pick the strongest project and make it installable and reproducible: pinned toolchain version, a container or documented environment, one command to build and flash.A fresh machine can build and flash it following only the README, with the toolchain version pinned.
28Documentation to product standard: architecture overview, a data-flow or state diagram, the interface specification, the measurement results, and a decisions log with the alternatives you rejected and why.A reader can explain your design back to you without asking a question, and the decisions log names real trade-offs rather than restating the code.
29Harden. Full error handling audit on every peripheral call, watchdog policy, recovery behaviour, and a written risk list of what you know is weak.Zero unchecked calls (verified by a script or a grep in CI), and a risk list that names at least three real weaknesses.
30Write the narrative: a short technical blog-style post explaining one non-obvious bug or trade-off from the project. Publish it where engineers will see it.A published post or internal write-up that a senior engineer would comment on rather than scroll past.

Risks and how this plan handles them

  • Course completion mistaken for competence. Nothing in this plan is satisfied by finishing a course. Every acceptance criterion names an artefact, a measurement or a reaction from another engineer.
  • Overrunning phases. If a phase runs long, cut its scope and keep the gate date. Gates are the enforcement mechanism for the whole plan.
  • Hardware blocking progress. Phase 5 stalls if the board or probe misbehaves. Mitigation: pick a board with a first-class Zephyr board page from day one, and order it in week 4.
  • Learning in isolation. Weeks 7, 23, 30 and 32 force contact with other engineers. Without review, you can hold a wrong mental model for six months without noticing.
  • Concurrency of life. 6 to 8 hours a week for 34 weeks is about 210 to 270 hours. Below 4 hours a week this becomes a three-year project and usually fails; if that is the real constraint, halve the scope and double the timeline deliberately rather than quietly.

What this plan cannot do

It cannot certify competence, and it does not constitute a documented development commitment from your employer. Movement into a specific team depends on headcount, business need and your manager's decision, none of which this plan controls. It also does not replace hands-on time on production firmware with real customers and real schedules; that only comes with the role, and the honest framing to a hiring manager is exactly that.

Plan assumes weeks 1 to 3 are completed as written and unchanged. Time budget 6 to 8 hours per week from week 4. Artefact and acceptance criteria are designed to be independently verifiable. Prepared 4 November 2026.
